Write a cover letter that explains why your experience fits

A useful cover letter connects your background to a particular job. It gives the reader a reason to look more closely at your résumé. Start with the work you have actually done, then explain which parts relate to the employer’s needs.

Start with the role and two relevant examples

Name the position you want. Choose one or two requirements from the announcement and match them to specific examples from your own work, education, projects, or volunteering. If you supported a process, say you supported it. Describe leadership only when you actually held that responsibility.

For example, an applicant who prepared weekly reports and helped schedule training could explain how those tasks relate to an operations role. The letter should name the work product and the contribution. It should not turn scheduling assistance into department management or add a result the applicant cannot verify.

What if you have no numbers?

You can write a clear example without a percentage or dollar amount. Describe what you did, what you used, who the work served, and any result you can support. “Prepared weekly reports in Excel for the coordinator” is more useful than an unsupported claim about increasing productivity. Add numbers only when you can explain where they came from.

How do you explain a career change?

Identify transferable work rather than claiming experience in the new occupation. A retail employee may have trained colleagues, maintained records, handled customer questions, or updated a spreadsheet. Those examples can show relevant skills without relabeling a retail job as an office-management position.

A gap in employment does not need an invented job. If an explanation is useful, keep it brief and truthful. Do not include private medical or family details merely to fill space.

Keep the letter distinct from the résumé

The résumé organizes your employment and qualifications. The letter explains the connection to this opportunity. Use a few short paragraphs: an opening that names the role, a focused discussion of relevant examples, and a professional closing. Avoid repeating every résumé bullet or praising company values you have not researched.

Check the announcement before submitting

Some employers require a letter, ask specific questions, or request another type of statement. Follow those instructions. For federal jobs, a cover letter does not replace the résumé, required documents, assessment, or specialized-experience evidence. See our federal résumé guide and the agency’s current instructions.

Review the final document

  • Does it name the correct role?
  • Are every employer, date, credential, duty, and result supported?
  • Does it explain a relevant connection rather than repeat a generic introduction?
  • Are responsibility levels and verb tenses consistent?
  • Is the letter readable on one page, with no placeholders or repeated signature?
